Leeds has quietly built one of the most complete beer scenes in Britain. Plenty of cities can offer you a strip of taprooms or a handful of grand old pubs; Leeds gives you both, often within a ten-minute walk of each other. Victorian drinking palaces sit alongside railway arches and converted mills, and the city's brewers range from heritage names to some of the most inventive independents in the country. If you would rather explore it with an expert, our [Leeds guide](/tours/leeds/) lists the tours and tastings we run in the city — but here is the lie of the land for anyone planning their own wander.

## Start in the city centre

The centre of Leeds is where old and new Leeds drink side by side. Whitelock's Ale House, founded in 1715 and generally agreed to be the oldest pub in the city, is the essential first stop: a long, narrow, gloriously preserved interior where the cask ale is taken seriously and the atmosphere does half the work. A few minutes away, North Bar is often credited as one of the country's first craft beer bars, and its influence on the city is hard to overstate — the team behind it went on to found North Brewing Co, whose beers you will find all over Leeds. Between the two you will pass enough good pubs and bars to fill an evening without ever crossing a main road.

## Holbeck and the south bank

Cross the river and you are in Leeds's brewing heartland, past and present. This was Tetley territory for the best part of two centuries — the former brewery's grand headquarters building still stands as a reminder of what was once one of Britain's biggest brewers — and the area around Brewery Wharf wears that history well. The modern story belongs to Northern Monk, which brews in a converted mill in Holbeck and has grown into one of the most recognisable names in UK craft beer. Its taproom, The Refectory, is a destination in its own right; check their site for current opening hours before you make the trip.

## North and west: Sheepscar, Meanwood and Kirkstall

Head north of the centre and the breweries get smaller and friendlier. Nomadic Beers is a proper independent doing things the hands-on way, and the stretch up towards Meanwood has become a quiet corridor of brewing activity. Out west along the River Aire, Kirkstall Brewery has built a loyal following for consistently excellent beer with a nod to the area's brewing past — the original Kirkstall Brewery was a Victorian giant, and the modern incarnation honours it well. Taproom opening times across all of these vary through the week, so check each brewery's site before setting out.

## Where the locals drink

Beyond the headline names, Leeds rewards curiosity. The city centre's arcades and side streets hide long-standing ale houses that predate the craft boom by a century; the area around the universities has a younger, hoppier crowd; and the suburbs — Headingley, Chapel Allerton, Horsforth — each support good beer pubs of their own. A sensible rule: if a Leeds pub has a well-kept cask line and a landlord who wants to talk about it, stay for another.

## Planning your Leeds beer weekend

A few practical notes. Taproom culture in Leeds is strongest from Thursday to Sunday, and several breweries only open their doors at the weekend — always check current hours before travelling. The city is compact and flat enough to do most of the centre on foot, and the train station puts you within fifteen minutes' walk of almost everything mentioned here. Pace yourself with halves and thirds where they are offered; Leeds brewers are not shy with their double IPAs.

However you do it — a self-guided amble through Holbeck, a tutored tasting with a beer expert, or a deep dive into three centuries of pub history — Leeds delivers one of the best beer weekends in the country. Come thirsty, and give yourself more time than you think you need.
