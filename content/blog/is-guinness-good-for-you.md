"Guinness is good for you." It is one of the most famous advertising lines ever written, and nearly a century on, people still half-believe it. Ask around any pub and someone will tell you that Guinness is full of iron, that it counts as a meal, or that their gran was told to drink a bottle a day by her doctor. So is there anything to it? The short answer: Guinness is a perfectly sensible pint — [around 210 calories](/blog/calories-in-pint-of-guinness/) and a modest 4.2% ABV — but the health halo around it is almost entirely marketing. Here's how the myth started, what's actually in the glass, and what an honest answer looks like.

## Where "Guinness is good for you" came from

The slogan dates back to 1929, when Guinness hired the London advertising agency S.H. Benson to run its first major campaign. The agency reportedly asked drinkers why they chose Guinness, and many replied that it made them feel good — so "Guinness is Good for You" went up on posters across Britain and Ireland. Artist John Gilroy later added the toucan, the pelican and the girder-carrying workman, and the campaign became one of the most recognisable in advertising history.

The remarkable thing is how far the idea travelled beyond the posters. For decades, Guinness was genuinely recommended in medical settings: to post-operative patients, to nursing mothers, and famously to blood donors in Ireland, who were handed a bottle after giving blood — a tradition that survived until surprisingly recently. None of this was based on rigorous evidence. It was based on the reasonable-sounding but wrong idea that a dark, malty, filling drink must be nourishing.

Modern advertising rules would never allow the claim today, and Diageo, which owns Guinness, no longer makes it. But slogans this good never quite die.

## The iron myth, measured

The most persistent version of the myth is that Guinness is rich in iron — hence the blood donors. There is a grain of truth: Guinness does contain iron, because barley does. But the amount is tiny. A pint provides somewhere around 0.3mg of iron. NHS guidance suggests men need about 8.7mg a day and women aged 19 to 49 about 14.8mg.

Do the arithmetic and you'd need roughly 30 pints a day to hit a man's iron requirement, and nearly 50 for a woman's. At that point iron would be the least of your worries. A bowl of fortified breakfast cereal, a portion of lentils or a serving of red meat delivers many times more iron than a pint of stout. If you're anaemic, the black stuff is not the answer.

## What's actually in a pint

Strip away the folklore and Guinness Draught is nutritionally unremarkable — in a good way. The figures below are approximate; recipes change, so check the label:

- **Calories:** around 210 per pint — mid-table for beer, and less than many people assume. We've broken this down fully in our [calories in a pint of Guinness](/blog/calories-in-pint-of-guinness/) guide.
- **Alcohol:** 4.2% ABV, which works out at about 2.4 units per pint — lower than most premium lagers and far below most craft IPAs.
- **Carbohydrates:** roughly 18g per pint, mostly unfermented malt sugars rather than added sugar — more on that in our guide to [carbs in beer](/blog/carbs-in-beer/).
- **Fat:** essentially none.
- **The rest:** small amounts of B vitamins, a little soluble fibre from the barley, and trace minerals.

That dense, creamy body, incidentally, comes from nitrogen rather than anything calorific. Guinness is served with nitrogen gas as well as carbon dioxide, which produces the tight, velvety head. It looks rich and tastes rich, but the darkness comes from a small proportion of roasted barley — not from extra sugar or strength. Plenty of golden, innocent-looking beers carry more calories and considerably more alcohol.

## The antioxidant claim — treat with caution

Every few years an article does the rounds claiming science has proven Guinness is good for your heart. Most trace back to a 2003 study from the University of Wisconsin, in which researchers found that Guinness reduced blood-clotting activity in dogs, while a pale lager did not. The suggested mechanism was flavonoids — antioxidant compounds from the barley, which dark beers contain more of than light ones.

It's a genuinely interesting finding, but it deserves scepticism. It was one small study, it was in dogs, and it has never been followed up with the kind of large human trials that would justify a health claim. Barley-derived antioxidants are real, but you'll get far more of them — without the alcohol — from fruit, vegetables, tea or coffee. No cardiologist is prescribing stout.

## So what's the honest answer?

By the standards of modern evidence, no alcoholic drink is "good for you". The UK Chief Medical Officers' guidance is that there's no completely safe level of drinking, which is why the advice caps regular consumption at 14 units a week for both men and women — about six pints of Guinness, ideally spread across the week with drink-free days.

But within the world of beer, Guinness holds up well. It's lower in alcohol than most of what sits alongside it on the bar, moderate in calories, and satisfying enough that many people drink it slowly and stop sooner. There's a reason [stout is booming right now](/blog/why-stout-is-booming-2026/): it delivers enormous flavour without demanding a big ABV. And if you want the ritual without the units, Guinness 0.0 is one of the better alcohol-free beers on the market — same nitrogen surge, roughly a third of the calories, and a fixture in our round-up of the [lowest calorie beers in the UK](/blog/lowest-calorie-beers-uk/).

One thing that definitely isn't good for you, in case you were wondering: the [Baby Guinness](/blog/baby-guinness/), the coffee-liqueur-and-Irish-cream shot dressed up to look like a tiny pint. It contains no Guinness whatsoever and rather more sugar than the real thing. Wonderful fun; terrible health food.

So enjoy the pint, skip the pseudo-science, and let the slogan be what it always was — a great line, not a prescription.
