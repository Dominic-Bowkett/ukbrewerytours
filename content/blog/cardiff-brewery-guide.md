Cardiff might be the most underrated beer city in Britain. The Welsh capital is compact, walkable and sociable to its core, with a castle in the middle, a stadium that turns the whole centre into a street party on match days, and a beer scene that has grown enormously in confidence over the past decade. Historic Welsh brewing sits alongside a lively band of independents, and you can cover most of it on foot in a weekend. If you would rather have someone show you around, our [Cardiff guide](/tours/cardiff/) has the details of our guided tasting tour — but here is what to know before you go.

## Brains and Cardiff's brewing heritage

You cannot talk about Cardiff breweries without starting with Brains. For well over a century the family firm was Welsh brewing, its name painted on pub signs across the city and its dark mild and SA bitter woven into Cardiff life. The brewing landscape has changed in recent years, but the Brains name remains everywhere in the capital, and plenty of the city's traditional pubs still pour it. It is the thread that connects modern Cardiff drinking to the generations before — and a reminder that this was a proper brewing city long before anyone said the words "craft beer".

## The new wave of Cardiff breweries

The modern scene is led by some genuinely good independents. Tiny Rebel, founded just along the coast in Newport, has become one of the best-known craft brewers in Wales — its beers turn up across the UK, and its city-centre Cardiff bar is a reliable first stop for anyone getting to know Welsh beer. Crafty Devil is the home-grown Cardiff independent, brewing with a cheerful punk streak and running its own neighbourhood bars; check their site for current opening hours. Add Zerodegrees, the city-centre brewpub where the tanks sit in full view of your table, and a supporting cast of small breweries and taprooms in the city's suburbs, and you have plenty to work through.

What do Welsh brewers do well? Honestly, most things — but the sweet spot is beer built for sociable drinking. Expect well-made pales and IPAs with plenty of character, proper dark beers that nod to the country's mild-drinking heritage, and a growing confidence with lagers and stouts. The scene is small enough that the brewers all know each other and big enough that there is always something new on the bar, which is exactly the balance you want as a visitor.

## Where to drink: pubs worth crossing town for

Cardiff's pub stock is one of its quiet strengths. The City Arms, in the shadow of the stadium, is the city's famous ale pub — a many-handpulled institution that gets gloriously chaotic on international days. Womanby Street, the little lane of music venues opposite the castle, is the heart of the city's independent nightlife and a good bet for finding Welsh beer on tap. And the Victorian arcades that lattice the city centre hide cafés, bottle shops and snug bars that reward a slow afternoon's exploring. As ever, opening hours shift through the week — check before you make a special trip.

## Making a weekend of it

Cardiff rewards a full weekend. Spend Saturday on the beer — castle, taprooms, a proper pub session — then walk it off on Sunday around Cardiff Bay or Bute Park, one of the finest city-centre parks in Britain. Pontcanna and Canton, just west of the centre, are the neighbourhoods to explore for good coffee, independent restaurants and a couple of the city's best-loved locals. Trains from London and Bristol make it an easy car-free trip, which matters when the itinerary is mostly beer.

## Cardiff beer FAQs, briefly

When should you visit? Any time, though be aware that rugby internationals transform the city — brilliant fun, but book everything early and expect the pubs to be heaving. Is it walkable? Almost entirely; the centre is flat and compact, and the castle-to-stadium-to-arcades circuit takes minutes, not hours. Do you need to book taprooms? Rarely, but smaller venues keep limited hours, so check their sites for current opening times before building your route around one.
